Trifecta frame results

Where food appears in historical Dutch texts (1500–1940), and in what role. Built .

How to read this

Rows are sentences that contain a food word, harvested from digitised books (mostly DBNL). Each row passed through an automatic three-step annotation (a local language model): A is this sentence about food at all? B which situation (frame) does it describe? C who and what fill the roles? A frame is a row that passed A and B with one of four situations:

  • Cooking: preparing food (boiling, baking, mixing)
  • Eating & drinking: someone consumes food
  • Cure: food used as a remedy or medicine
  • Preserving: keeping food (pickling, drying, sugaring, salting)

Yield is the share of rows that turned out to be a frame; the rest mention food in passing (a name, a metaphor, a price) or were filtered out early.

Sources are successive harvests, each sampled differently:

  • Curated corpus: 61 hand-selected cookbooks, household and medical books, harvested in depth
  • Initial exploration: an early hand-picked batch (extra DBNL works, colonial books from Leiden, KB cookbooks, Google Books)
  • DBNL sweep: all of DBNL up to 1920, at most 10 sentences per work
  • Entry scope: the entries of recipe and household dictionaries (e.g. Chomel), read entry by entry
  • Weak-regime verb: works outside cookbooks (literature, travel, uncatalogued), sentences with a cooking or eating verb, at most 50 per work
  • Indies terms: Indies-cuisine words (ketoembar, santen, sambal…) in 21 cookbooks and household books 1790–1940, at most 80 per book; here the interest is which word is used, more than the frame

Genre is assigned per work: our own per-work labels first, then the library catalogues (DBNL's genre for the work, the KB's GGC classification). Periodicals are yearbooks and journal volumes, which mix genres. Other non-fiction is history, religion, language and the like. Unknown means no catalogue gave a usable genre.

Read with care:

  • Sources are samples with per-work caps, not a balanced corpus: compare yield and shares, not raw counts between sources.
  • A single big work can dominate: the Huishoudelyk woordboek (Chomel, 1743) alone gives over 3,000 frames, split over two sources. Switch Count to Works to see past it.
  • Years are edition years, not dates of composition; undated rows are left out of the decade chart.
  • All labels are automatic and not hand-checked; treat small numbers as indications.
