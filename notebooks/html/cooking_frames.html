Cooking in Dutch print, 1500–1940: a frame-oriented look¶

Every food mention in the corpus was annotated with a frame: what the text does with the food.

frame the food is … frame elements kept here
COOKING_CREATION prepared Food_Product (what is made), Method (how)
INGESTION eaten or drunk
CURE used as medicine Affliction, Food_Treatment
PRESERVING kept

Questions: which frames dominate in which kinds of book and when; what is cooked and how; which foods move between the kitchen, the table and the apothecary; how the words for cooking change.

Read before trusting a number

  • The annotations are made by a language model (with checks), not by hand. Frame elements are only kept when their words actually occur in the passage ("grounded"). Where the model gives a Food_Product or Method, about 9 and 8 in 10 are grounded; but only about 4 in 10 cooking mentions end up with a usable product or method, so B–D describe that subset.
  • The corpus is a sample, and uneven over time; dates are edition years; genres come from our own rules or, where those fail, from library catalogues (column genre_source).
  • A mention counts once, whatever the quantity.

Reading it. Cookbooks (RECIPE_PRACTICE) are what you would expect: mostly cooking, with a solid share of cure (recipes for the sick, home remedies) and most of the preserving. Medical books are nearly all cure and cooking (preparing a remedy is cooking). Literary and periodical texts are about eating and drinking. Over time the share of cure falls, the share of ingestion rises: read with the counts below the chart, since the early periods are small.

cook = f[f.frame == "COOKING_CREATION"].copy()
def grounded(row, key):
    v = row.elements.get(key)
    return v.lower() if isinstance(v, str) and v and v.lower() in str(row.context).lower() else None

C. How: the methods, and their words¶

The Method element, normalised to a verb (hand-made list below: gekookt, kookt, kooken → koken). The list is short on purpose; anything not on it stays other. Note sieden / zieden (gesoden): the older word for boiling.

In [6]:
METHODS = [  # (verb, pattern on the Method words); first match wins
    ("sieden", r"\b(?:ge)?[sz](?:ie|ee)d|\bgesoden|\bzood"),
    ("koken", r"\b(?:ge|door)?k(?:oo|o)k"),
    ("braden", r"\b(?:ge)?braa?d|\bbraet"),
    ("bakken", r"\b(?:ge)?bac?kk?(?:en|t)?\b|\bgebacken"),
    ("stoven", r"\b(?:ge)?stoo?v"),
    ("roosteren", r"rooster"),
    ("smelten", r"smelt"),
    ("mengen", r"\b(?:ge)?meng"),
    ("stampen", r"stamp"),
    ("snijden", r"\b(?:ge)?sn(?:ij|y|e)d|\bsned"),
    ("weken", r"\b(?:ge)?wee?k(?:en|t)\b"),
    ("trekken", r"\btrek|\bgetrokken"),
    ("distilleren", r"distill|distil"),
    ("branden", r"\b(?:ge)?brand"),
    ("brouwen", r"brouw"),
]
def verb(s):
    if not isinstance(s, str):
        return None
    for v, p in METHODS:
        if re.search(p, s):
            return v
    return "other"
cook["method"] = cook["method_raw"].map(verb)
cook["method"].value_counts().to_frame("mentions").T

Reading it. Sieden is the main boiling word of the earliest books (most of the few methods before 1600, 41 in 1650–1699) and fades as koken takes over (274 in 1700–1749): the same act, a new word. Braden and bakken are there throughout; distilleren and trekken (infusing) cluster in 1650–1749, the age of household remedies in recipe form. Mind the counts: the periods before 1650 are small.

Reading it. Tea is the clearest case: a third of its mentions before 1700 are medicinal, after 1750 only 6%. Brandy, cloves, milk, citrus and olive oil follow the same way, from the apothecary to the kitchen and the table. The table lists the foods whose share of cure mentions drops most between the seventeenth and the later eighteenth century. Read the passages before believing a shift: a change of genre in the sample (more cookbooks, fewer herbals) moves every food the same way, so compare with section A.
